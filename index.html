Part 1

Design, at a computer

Nothing physical happens yet. You are deciding exactly what DNA to order.

1

Confirm the guide sequences

What you're using

crRNA oligos
Short DNA sequences that will become the — the part that tells Cas13a what to destroy. Someone already designed these, so you are checking rather than creating.
T7 reference genome
The published, officially numbered T7 sequence, NC_001604.1. The coordinate system, so that everyone means the same thing by "position 29064".
CK1A-1 consensus
The actual ancestral genome of your evolution experiment. Its numbering runs one or two bases off the reference, which is normal.
Geneious Prime
Sequence software. Opens genomes, stacks sequences on top of each other to compare them, and edits sequences.

Do this

  • Open the crRNA sequences in Geneious.
  • Align each against both the T7 reference genome and the CK1A-1 consensus.
  • Confirm the sequence matches CK1A-1 exactly across the whole spacer.
  • Confirm the four-base are AAAC on oligo 1 and AGCA on oligo 2.
  • Confirm oligo 2 is the complement of oligo 1 everywhere except those overhangs.

Why

The guide's whole job is to recognise unedited phage so Cas13a can destroy them. It therefore has to match the ancestral sequence, not the edited one. One wrong base and it either fails to recognise them or targets somewhere else entirely.

The overhangs are what make the oligo pair slot into the plasmid the right way round in step 7. Wrong overhangs and nothing assembles.

2

Design the donor sequences

What you're using

HR donor
The piece of DNA carrying your mutation. HR is homologous recombination, the process that copies it into the phage. This is the thing you are designing.
Table of evolving sites
The list from the evolution experiment showing every site that changed and the amino acid change each causes. Use it to confirm your edit does what you think.
Codon usage table
Sharp et al. 1985, for T7. Tells you which of the several codons for an amino acid E. coli reads most easily.

Do this

  • Pull roughly 100 bases either side of your target base out of CK1A-1 and map it to the reference genome with translation displayed.
  • Change the target base. 29064 C→T gives A247V; 29066 T→C gives F248L. Confirm against the table of evolving sites.
  • Add to the three before and three after. Leave the target codon alone.
  • Choose ; where two are equally preferred take the transition, A↔G or C↔T.
  • Trim to exactly 52 bases past the outermost change each way — the .
  • Take the reverse complement as the second strand.
  • Add TATC to the front of the forward strand, TCCT to the front of the reverse.

Why

Three jobs happening in one piece of DNA, and they are easy to confuse.

The target base is the mutation you care about. Its codon is left unrecoded so the amino acid change you intended is the only coding change you get.

The synonymous changes are a disguise. Your guide matches the ancestral sequence, so if you changed only the target base the guide would still grip the edited phage and destroy it during enrichment. Six nearby codons changed silently means the guide can no longer recognise it, while the protein stays identical apart from your one intended change.

They are not assumed harmless. T7's codon usage tracks how abundant each E. coli tRNA is, so synonymous sites here are under real selection. Picking preferred codons biases any effect toward neutral rather than deleterious.

The 52-base arms are handles. They are identical to the phage genome, which is what lets the phage find this DNA and copy from it. No matching arms, no recombination.

Because 29064 and 29066 sit two bases apart, one design covers both and all three constructs come out of the same recoded window. For the double, the window runs from three codons before the first edit to three codons after the second.

3

Order

What you're using

Oligos
Short pieces of synthetic single-stranded DNA, made to order. They arrive as an invisible dried pellet.
IDT
Integrated DNA Technologies. Delivery is usually a few days.

Do this

  • Order four per construct — two for the guide, two for the donor.
  • Before submitting, map forward against reverse once more. Identical and overlapping, except TATC at the start of the forward and AGGA at the end of the reverse.

Why

Four and not two, because DNA is double-stranded. Each functional piece needs both strands, which you pair up in step 6.

That last alignment is your final chance to catch a design error. After it you spend money and about a week of bench time before a mistake would reveal itself.

Building the plasmids

Turning ordered DNA into bacteria that carry it.

4

Purify the empty vectors

What you're using

pBA559
A carrying Cas13a and an empty slot for a guide. Chloramphenicol resistant. Addgene #186235.
pBA707
A plasmid with an empty slot for a donor. Kanamycin resistant. Addgene #186238.
Glycerol stocks
Frozen tubes of bacteria carrying each plasmid. The stops ice crystals from killing the cells.
Chloramphenicol, kanamycin
. Each plasmid resists one of them, so adding it kills any cell that lost the plasmid.
QIAprep miniprep kit
Buffers and spin columns that pull plasmid DNA out of bacteria. P1 holds the cells, P2 bursts them, N3 makes the chromosome clump and drop out while the plasmid stays dissolved, PB and PE wash, EB releases the DNA.
Agarose gel
A jelly you push DNA through with electricity. Small pieces travel further, so it shows whether you have DNA and roughly what shape.

Do this

  • Scrape each glycerol stock with a sterile loop without letting it thaw. Streak pBA559 onto , pBA707 onto . 37 °C, 24 h.
  • Loop one into 3 mL plus 3 µL of the matching 1000× antibiotic. Shake overnight at 37 °C.
  • Pellet 1.5 mL of culture at 8,000 rpm, 3 min.
  • Resuspend in 250 µL P1. Add 250 µL P2, invert to mix. Add 350 µL N3, invert. Do not vortex.
  • Spin 15,800 rpm, 10 min. Move 800 µL of supernatant to a spin column, avoiding the pellet.
  • Wash with 500 µL PB, then 750 µL PE. Spin dry again with the flow-through discarded.
  • Elute in 50 µL EB after standing 1 min. Store at 4 °C.
  • Run 5 µL on a 0.8% gel at 90 V for 45–60 min.

Why

This has to come first, because the Golden Gate reaction in step 7 needs purified plasmid as an ingredient. You cannot paste an insert into a vector you have not got in a tube.

What you are purifying is the empty vector, exactly as it arrived. This is the only miniprep in the whole workflow — once, here, never again.

The buffers each do one job, and N3 is the one that actually separates anything. It is high-salt, which makes the bacterial chromosome clump and fall out of solution while your small circular plasmid stays dissolved. That is why you must not vortex after it: shearing the chromosome releases fragments that come through the column with your plasmid.

The dry spin matters too. Residual ethanol from PE will inhibit every enzyme you use downstream.

Expect three or four bands on the gel — supercoiled, nicked and linear forms of the same plasmid. Normal, not contamination.

5

Make up the oligos

What you're using

Your delivered oligos
Dried pellets. Invisible, and light enough to sit on the inside of the cap.
Nuclease-free water
Water with no DNA-chewing enzymes in it. Ordinary water would slowly destroy your oligos.

Do this

  • Spin the tubes 30 s before opening them.
  • Dissolve to 100 µM — 40 µL water for a 4 nmol order, 250 µL for 25 nmol. Vortex, spin again.
  • Make a 1 µM working stock: 2 µL of the 100 µM into 198 µL water.
  • Label and store both at −20 °C.

Why

The volume you add is what sets the concentration, so getting it right here determines everything downstream.

Spin before opening because the pellet is invisible and can be stuck to the cap. Open without spinning and you can lose the whole order without ever seeing it happen.

Two concentrations because of risk. The 100 µM tube is your archive and you barely touch it. The 1 µM tube is what you pipette from daily, so contaminating or finishing it costs almost nothing.

6

Phosphorylate and anneal

What you're using

T4 PNK
Polynucleotide kinase. Sticks a phosphate group onto the end of DNA.
10× T4 ligase buffer
Used here instead of PNK buffer because it contains the ATP the kinase needs as fuel.
Thermocycler
A programmable heat block. Holds tubes at set temperatures and can ramp slowly between them.

Do this

  • One PCR tube, 50 µL: 5 µL of each 1 µM oligo, 5 µL 10× ligase buffer, 1 µL , 34 µL water.
  • Run 37 °C / 30 min, 65 °C / 20 min, 100 °C / 5 min, then ramp to 25 °C over 15 min.

Why

Two separate things happen in this one tube, which is why the program has four stages.

The 37 °C hold is phosphorylation. The kinase adds a phosphate to each oligo end, and physically cannot join DNA without it. Skip this and nothing assembles in step 7 — and you would not find out until you saw empty plates.

The 100 °C and the ramp are . The boil rips the strands fully apart; cooling slowly lets them find each other and zip together straight. Cool too fast and they pair crooked or fold back on themselves, and crooked duplexes will not ligate.

The 65 °C hold kills the kinase so it cannot interfere with the ligation you are about to set up.

7

Golden Gate assembly

What you're using

Restriction enzyme
A protein that cuts DNA at one specific sequence. opens pBA559, opens pBA707. Each leaves overhangs matching your oligos.
T4 DNA ligase
Seals DNA into a continuous strand. The glue.
No-oligo control
The same reaction with your insert left out. Measures how many colonies come from plasmid that never got cut or closed back up empty.

Do this

  • Mix 25 µL: 50–100 ng plasmid from step 4, 2.5 µL 10× ligase buffer, 0.5 µL T4 ligase, 1.5 µL restriction enzyme, 1 µL annealed oligos, water to volume.
  • Guide oligos into pBA559 with BsaI-HFv2. Donor oligos into pBA707 with BbsI.
  • Set up a alongside every assembly — identical, minus the oligos.
  • Run 30 cycles of 37 °C / 5 min and 16 °C / 5 min, then 60 °C / 5 min, then hold 4 °C.

Why

Cut and paste in one tube. The restriction enzyme opens the plasmid, the ligase glues your insert into the gap, and both run at once — which is why the 30 cycles alternate between the temperature the cutter prefers and the one the glue prefers. The reaction keeps retrying until it succeeds. The 60 °C at the end kills the cutter so it cannot reopen what you just built.

What changes in this step. Before it you have two separate things: a short linear piece of DNA with sticky ends, and a closed circular plasmid. After it you have one circle with your sequence built into it. The sticky ends are directional, so the insert can only go in one way round.

The tube is not pure, though. Some plasmids assembled correctly, some never got cut, some got cut and closed back up with nothing inside.

That is what the no-oligo control is for, and it is the one thing people skip. Since you are not sequencing, this control is your only evidence the insert went in at all. If it grows as many colonies as your real plate, every colony you pick is a coin flip. Write the counts down.

8

Transform

What you're using

NEB 10-beta competent cells
E. coli treated so they will take up loose DNA. , sold frozen, useless once warmed or shaken hard.
Outgrowth medium
Rich food for the recovery period after heat shock. LB works too.
Sterile glass beads
Poured onto a plate and shaken to spread liquid evenly, instead of a spreader.

Do this

  • Thaw cells on ice 10 min. 25 µL per transformation into labelled tubes.
  • Add 1 µL assembly. Flick four or five times. Never vortex. Ice 30 min, undisturbed.
  • at exactly 42 °C for exactly 30 s. Do not mix. Ice 5 min.
  • Add 950 µL room-temperature outgrowth medium. 37 °C, 250 rpm, 60 min.
  • Meanwhile warm selective plates to 37 °C and set up dilution tubes with 198 µL LB each.
  • Dilute to 10⁻², then 10⁻⁴. Spread 50 µL of each with beads. 37 °C overnight.
  • Plate the no-oligo control the same way, same antibiotic.

Why

Bacteria do not normally take up loose DNA. The cold, then the sudden heat, makes the membrane briefly permeable so the plasmid slips inside.

30 s is exact because it sits on a knife edge: longer starts killing cells, shorter does not open them.

No vortexing at any point. Competent cells shear easily and one hard mix can kill the whole tube.

The 60 min recovery is not optional. It lets surviving cells switch on the plasmid's resistance gene before they meet the antibiotic. Plate them straight away and even successful transformants die.

Two dilutions because transformation efficiency varies by orders of magnitude. One of them will give countable, separable colonies.

9

Purify the transformants and bank them

What you're using

80% glycerol
Mixed 1:1 into culture before freezing, giving 40% final. At that concentration the tube never freezes solid.
Cryovials or screw-cap tubes
Snap-cap tubes can pop open in deep cold.

Do this

  • Re-streak an isolated colony onto a fresh selective plate. 37 °C, 24 h.
  • Loop a single colony into 3 mL LB plus 3 µL antibiotic. Shake overnight.
  • Combine 500 µL culture with 500 µL 80% glycerol. Three replicates, labelled 1, 2, 3.
  • Store at −20 °C.

Why

The first plate has your transformant growing right next to untransformed neighbours it was sitting on. Re-streaking separates one cell's descendants from everything else, so the colony you pick is genuinely one clone.

Glycerol stops ice crystals from forming and shredding the cells. The 40% final concentration is deliberate for −20 °C: at that concentration the stock never freezes solid, so you can scrape without thawing. The 15–25% recipes are the ones paired with −80 °C.

Three replicates because freezers fail and tubes get dropped. Mix before pulling your 500 µL, and flick a tube that has sat a long time, since a partly liquid stock lets cells settle. Under-sampling is forgiving — reviving needs a few hundred cells out of roughly a billion per millilitre.

This ends Part 2, and you have no sequence confirmation. Everything downstream assumes the plasmids are correct. A mis-assembled donor would either produce no edits or the wrong edit, and the first sign would be failed plates weeks from now. The no-oligo counts from step 7 are your only evidence.

High-titer lysate

What you're using

K-12
Ordinary lab E. coli. No plasmid, no antibiotic needed, and every phage grows on it. The neutral host for everything.
K-12 aliquot
A 50 µL pre-portioned tube from −80 °C. This goes in the flask.
K-12 overnight
A fresh tube grown overnight, made the day before. This goes in the lawn. Different preparation, different job.
T7Δ2 and T7+
Your two ancestral phage. Viruses: they do nothing alone and only replicate inside a living host cell.
T7 storage buffer
A salt solution that keeps phage stable. Diluent instead of LB when you do not want anything growing.
Top agar
LB with only 0.7% agar, so it sets soft. Melted in a heat block, mixed with bacteria and phage, poured as a thin layer over a normal plate. The softness is what lets plaques spread.
Chloroform
Dissolves bacterial membranes and kills them. Phage have no membrane, so they survive untouched.

You need a lot of phage up front, because every later step throws most of it away.

Phage only replicate inside growing bacteria, which is why the K-12 gets a 60 min head start. Add phage to a culture that is not actively dividing and very little happens.

Efficiency of plaquing

What you're using

Killer host
10-beta carrying your pBA559 construct — Cas13a plus the guide. Destroys phage that still carry the ancestral sequence. Also called the crRNA host.
Empty-pBA559 host
Your control from step 7. Carries Cas13a but no guide. Essential here, not a cloning leftover.
aTc
Anhydrotetracycline. The chemical switch that turns Cas13a on. Light sensitive — keep it in foil.
EOP
Efficiency of plaquing. Titer on a restrictive host divided by titer on a permissive one. A low number means strong blocking.

The day before. Overnights of the and of the empty-pBA559 control, both 3 mL LB plus 3 µL chloramphenicol, no aTc. Plus a K-12 overnight.

EOP calculator

Enter the two titers, or the plaque counts and I will work the titers out.

Why

This is the check on your killer host before you depend on it. Unedited phage should barely grow there, so EOP should come out very low. If it comes out near 1, Cas13a is not working — wrong plasmid, no aTc, dead guide — and step 14 would enrich nothing at all.

It also sets the ceiling on enrichment. An EOP of 10⁻⁴ means roughly 10,000-fold selection against unedited phage per round, which tells you whether one round is enough.

What the empty pBA559 is for. It carries Cas13a but no guide, which separates two explanations for a low EOP. If your real killer host blocks hard and the empty one does not, your guide is doing the work. If both block equally hard, Cas13a is killing phage non-specifically and the enrichment in step 14 will not be specific either.

Given that you are not sequencing, this step carries extra weight. It is the only functional evidence your guide construct is correct.

Run this on both ancestors. If one gives a high EOP, the guide does not match that genome and editing in it will fail — much better to learn that here than after six editing runs.

Grow on the donor host

What you're using

Donor host
10-beta carrying pBA707 with your HR donor in it. The bacteria that hand the mutation to the phage. You have three, one per donor.
Empty-pBA707 host
Your other control from step 7. Phage grown on it go through the same process with no donor present, so anything that later survives the killer host must be an escape mutant.
96-well plate
A tray of small wells, used with a multichannel pipette so you can make eight dilutions at once.

The day before. Overnights of each in 3 mL LB plus 3 µL kanamycin, and one K-12 overnight.

Do this

  • 10 mL LB per flask, one flask per donor and ancestor pair.
  • 1 mL of the matching donor-host overnight into each. The uninfected control gets 1 mL K-12 overnight instead.
  • Shake 1 h at 37 °C; another 30 min if not cloudy.
  • 10 µL of the matching ancestral phage into each. Nothing in the control. Label donor and ancestor both.

  • Dilute across a 96-well row — 10 µL into 90 µL LB, eight steps, fresh tips every step.

Flask planner

Why

This is where the edit actually happens, and the mechanism is worth being clear about. Inside each infected cell, the phage genome and the donor plasmid are in the same space. The 52-base arms on the donor are identical to the phage genome, which lets the two line up, and sometimes the phage copies the plasmid's middle section — including your mutation — into itself. That is : copy-paste guided by matching sequence.

It is rare. Most phage come out completely unchanged, so what you harvest is mostly ancestral with a small edited fraction mixed in. Expected, not a failure. Separating them is the next two steps' job.

Kanamycin goes in the overnight because pBA707 carries kanamycin resistance. Without it, a cell that drops the plasmid grows just as fast as one that keeps it, and by morning a large share of your culture would have no donor at all — you would be growing phage on a host with nothing to give them.

Fresh tips at every dilution step, because carrying over a trace of the previous more concentrated well ruins every dilution after it.

Harvest the mix

What you're using

LB + kanamycin plates
The donor host needs kanamycin to keep its plasmid, so even the plates carry it.
Lacy plate
The stage just before a lawn clears completely — plaques merged, surviving bacteria looking like a net.
Cell scraper or loop
For lifting the whole soft top agar layer off the plate into a tube.

Do this

  • Spot 2 µL of each dilution onto LB + kanamycin plates overlaid with top agar holding 100 µL donor-host overnight and 3 µL kanamycin.
  • 5 min to set, invert, incubate.
  • Find the plate that has gone . Reproducibly around 10⁻⁵.
  • Scrape the whole plate into 3 mL LB or T7 storage buffer.
  • Add chloroform and vortex.

Why

You want the plate where the phage have eaten nearly all the lawn but not quite. That is the highest yield available while the bacteria were still alive long enough for recombination to have happened.

Too dilute and you get a few isolated plaques, nowhere near enough phage to carry forward. Too concentrated and the lawn died before much recombination occurred, so yield is high but the edited fraction is low.

You scrape the whole plate rather than picking plaques because you cannot tell by looking which phage are edited. Take all of them and let selection sort it out.

14

Enrich on the killer host

What you're using

Killer host
Same construct as step 11 — Cas13a plus your guide, on chloramphenicol.
aTc, in the top agar only
Turns Cas13a on at the moment of plating, not during growth.
Enrichment
Using selection to raise the proportion of what you want. You cannot pick edited phage out by hand, so you kill the unedited ones instead.

Do this

  • Grow the killer-host overnight in LB plus chloramphenicol only. No aTc while growing.
  • Dilute the scraped lysate 10⁻¹ through 10⁻⁷.
  • Spot 2 µL of each onto plates overlaid with top agar holding 100 µL killer-host overnight, 3 µL chloramphenicol and 3 µL 1000× aTc, about 5 nM final.
  • 5 min to set, invert, incubate 12–16 h.

Why

This is the filter, and the step the whole design turns on. Cas13a and the guide destroy any phage still carrying the ancestral sequence. Edited phage survive because their recoded window means the guide cannot grip. You go in with a mix and come out with mostly edited.

aTc is withheld during growth on purpose. Running an active nuclease while you are just growing cells selects for cells that break or silence the system. You turn it on in the plating agar, at the moment you need it.

If the induced culture clears slowly or not at all, that is consistent with enrichment working rather than failing. Most of the phage you added are being destroyed, so there is little left to replicate. Score isolated plaques, not liquid clearing.
15

Pick plaques

What you're using

Escape mutant
A phage that survived the killer host without your edit, by mutating the guide's target site on its own. Looks identical on a plate to a real edit.
Empty-pBA707 lysate
From step 12. Your measured escape background, since nothing in it could have been edited.

Do this

  • Cut out isolated plaques from the enrichment plates.
  • Propagate each on the killer host to make a working lysate.
  • Spot plate to titer each on both the killer host and K-12.
  • Plate the empty-pBA707 lysate on the killer host in parallel, as the escape background.

Why

A plaque on the killer host means that phage survived. It does not mean it is the phage you designed.

A phage can also survive by mutating the guide's target site on its own, with no help from your donor. That is an , and on a plate it is indistinguishable from a real edit.

Without sequencing you cannot tell them apart directly, which makes the empty-pBA707 control load-bearing. Phage grown on a host with no donor cannot have been edited, so every plaque it produces on the killer host is an escape mutant by definition. That gives you a background rate — if your donor plates give far more plaques than the empty-donor plates, the excess is real editing.

It is a rate and not an identification. You will know roughly what fraction of your plaques are genuine, but not which individual isolate is which. Worth raising with your lab if the fitness numbers come out strange.

Part 4

Measuring fitness

A . Three constructs across two backgrounds is six separate races.

16

Mix one to one

What you're using

Edited lysates
From step 15, one per donor and ancestor pair.
Ancestral freezer stocks
The two glycerol stocks you banked in step 10. The same tubes for every race.

Do this

  • Mix each edited lysate 1:1 by titer with the ancestral lysate of its own background.
  • Plate the mixture before any transfer, to measure the actual starting ratio.

Why

Fitness only means anything relative to something else, so you run a race.

Starting at 1:1 leaves the most room to detect a shift in either direction. Start at 99:1 and a small effect is invisible against the noise.

The starting ratio gets measured, not assumed. Your two lysates were titered separately, possibly on different days, so the real mix is never exactly 1:1.

Never cross the backgrounds. Racing an edited T7+ against a T7Δ2 ancestor measures the difference between two strains, not the effect of your mutation, and the number would be meaningless. The single easiest way to ruin the experiment, and it looks like a labelling slip rather than an error.
17

Serial transfer

What you're using

Serial transfer
Growing a population, then using a little of it to start the next round, repeatedly. The same thing the original evolution experiment did for 250 rounds.
Replicates
Independent repeats of the same race, run side by side. Without them you have no error bars.

Do this

  • 10 µL of mixed lysate into 10 mL of exponentially growing host. Incubate until lysis.
  • Carry that lysate forward as the inoculum for the next round.
  • Repeat for your chosen number of transfers, with independent replicates of every construct.

Why

One round of growth is not enough to see a small difference. Each transfer compounds it — 5% better per round becomes countable after ten rounds.

10 µL into 10 mL is a thousand-fold dilution each round, which keeps the phage growing exponentially instead of running out of hosts and plateauing.

Replicates because any single race can go oddly by chance. Without them you cannot say whether a difference is real or noise.

18

Score on mixed lawns

What you're using

Mixed host lawn
Top agar containing both K-12 and the killer host. The trick the whole assay depends on.
Turbid and clear plaques
The two readouts. Turbid means the phage could only kill some of the bacteria there; clear means it killed everything.

Do this

  • Plate on a lawn mixing K-12 with the killer host, aTc in the top agar.
  • Count turbid and clear plaques separately, before and after the transfers.
  • Count at a dilution giving 30–300 plaques.

Why

This is the clever part of the whole design. The lawn is two bacteria at once: K-12, which both phage infect productively, and the killer host, which only edited phage infect productively.

An ancestral phage lands and can only eat the K-12 cells, so it clears a patchy cloudy hole — turbid. An edited phage eats everything, so it clears a clean transparent hole — clear. Both genotypes counted on the same plate, by eye, with no sequencing needed.

30–300 is the countable window. Below it, random variation swamps the count. Above it, plaques merge.

Calculate

What you're using

R
The ratio of edited phage to ancestral phage. R₀ before transfers, Rₜ after.
s
Selection coefficient. How fast one genotype beats another, per round.
W
Relative fitness. The same thing as a multiplier, where 1 is a tie.
ε
Epistasis. How far the double mutant departs from what the two singles predict.

s = ln(Rt / R0) / t

W = es

ε = sAB − (sA + sB)

ε = WAB − (WA × WB)

Epistasis calculator

One background at a time. Never mix T7Δ2 numbers with T7+ numbers.

Why

Why the log. Growth is multiplicative, not additive. Taking logs turns compounding growth into a straight line, so the slope is a single clean number and the assumption of a constant selection coefficient becomes visible as how straight the fit is. Estimate from the slope across all your time points, not from two.

Why both scales. Additive and multiplicative can disagree in sign for large effects, and choosing between them is a modelling assumption rather than something the data decides.

Why ε is the whole point. You measure each single mutant and the double, all against the same ancestor, then ask whether the double performs the way the two singles predict. If 29064 alone gives +5% and 29066 alone gives +5%, additivity predicts about +10%. Measure less and they interfere — negative epistasis, which is what a looks like. Measure more and they help each other.

All three race the ancestor and never each other. The mixed-lawn trick can only distinguish edited from ancestral, and the double mutant carries both recoded windows so it escapes either guide — indistinguishable from a single mutant on the same plate. A common opponent is what puts all three genotypes on one scale.

Two epistasis values, one per background. Compute ε separately for T7Δ2 and T7+, using only the constructs built in that background, and do not pool them. Agreement between the two is a far stronger claim than one number alone — and disagreement is itself a finding, since it would be direct evidence that the interaction depends on genetic context.
